    Rechargeable Batteries

    4 min read

    A clean energy economy needs batteries that store more energy, more safely and at lower cost than today’s Li-ion cells, and that are made from abundant elements. Our work spans sodium-ion batteries, all-solid-state batteries and their solid electrolytes, and multivalent batteries, and, across all three, the interphases, interfaces and surfaces where their components meet. We use first-principles calculations, statistical mechanics and machine learning, often together with experimental collaborators, to understand and design electrodes, electrolytes and the interfaces between them.

    Sodium-ion batteries. Sodium is abundant and evenly distributed around the world, which makes sodium-ion batteries an inexpensive alternative to Li-ion, but their energy density is limited by the positive electrode. We have charted the stability and voltages of 28 NaSICON phosphate positive electrodes,1 helped show that single-phase NaxV2(PO4)3 electrodes raise the average operating voltage to about 3.70 V,2 and found that replacing half of the vanadium in the fluorophosphate NaxV2(PO4)2F3 with molybdenum lowers the voltage for extracting its third sodium ion by 0.6 V, raising its theoretical capacity from about 128 to 174 mAh g−1.3 On the negative electrode, we have mapped the phase diagram of sodium–lead alloys and their sodium-alloying voltages.4

    All-solid-state batteries and solid electrolytes. Solid-state batteries (see Figure 1) promise greater safety, energy density and cycle life, chiefly because they do without the flammable liquid electrolytes of Li-ion cells. Making them practical requires solid electrolytes that conduct ions quickly, remain stable against both electrodes, work with metal anodes, and keep physical contact with the electrodes over many cycles.5


    Figure 1: Representation of a bipolar-stacked solid-state battery.5

    To understand fast ion conduction, we combined more than 2,000 kinetic Monte Carlo simulations with impedance measurements to map Na-ion transport across the NaSICON electrolytes Na1+xZr2SixP3−xO12,6 synthesized zirconia-free NaSICON with a room-temperature conductivity of about 3.5 mS cm−1,7 and showed how stacking faults, set by synthesis, control Li-ion conduction in the halide Li3YCl6.8 A good solid electrolyte must also block electrons: we have predicted the electronic conductivity that native defects give solid electrolytes such as Li6PS5Cl and Na3PS4, a source of degradation and short circuits.9

    Multivalent batteries. Multivalent ions such as magnesium and calcium carry two charges each and are far more abundant than lithium, so batteries built on them promise high energy densities at low cost. Their high charge, however, makes them move slowly through electrode materials, and plating and stripping them at the metal anode remains difficult.10 Interest in multivalent electrochemistry has grown rapidly (see Figure 2).


    Figure 2: Number of publications from 1985 until 2015 featuring multivalent electrochemistry.11

    For calcium batteries, we have identified borate compounds in the solid–electrolyte interphase that let calcium plate reversibly, and showed that tuning how the electrolyte solvates the calcium ions speeds up plating tenfold.12 Screening large chemical spaces, we have found new candidate positive electrodes for calcium, such as the post-spinel CaV2O4 and the layered CaNb2O4,13 and singled out the NaSICON frameworks CaxV2(PO4)3, CaxMn2(SO4)3 and CaxFe2(SO4)3 as promising calcium cathodes.14 For magnesium–sulfur batteries, we have traced how charge moves through the insulating discharge products MgS2 and MgS.15

    Interphases, interfaces and surfaces. Whether a battery lasts is often decided where its components meet: electrolytes decompose against the electrodes and form interphases, ions slow down as they cross interfaces and grain boundaries, and exposed surfaces react with their surroundings. These regions are the hardest parts of a battery to model, and we develop and apply first-principles approaches to describe them.16,17

    At the metal anode, we have shown how electronic passivation stops the growth of the interphase between lithium metal and the LiPON electrolyte,18 and assessed the stability of more than 20 interfaces between lithium or sodium metal and the decomposition products of thiophosphate electrolytes, using machine-learned potentials to trace where the resistance to Li-ion transport arises.19 Coatings can protect the electrodes: we have revealed which phases of Li–Nb–O and Li–Ta–O coatings conduct lithium ions,20 and identified coating materials for magnesium batteries, such as MgSiN2 and the magnesium halides against the Mg-metal anode, and MgAl2O4 and Mg(PO3)2 against oxide cathodes.21

    In polycrystalline solid electrolytes, grain boundaries can slow Li-ion conduction by one to two orders of magnitude, as in the perovskite Li3xLa2/3−xTiO3,22 although they hinder transport far less in the sulfide Na3PS4 than in the oxide Na3PO4.23 From a library of 590 surfaces and grain boundaries of 11 solid electrolytes, we found that grain boundaries are weaker than the bulk, so particles tend to crack there.24 We have also shown how H2O and CO2 from air react with the surface of the garnet electrolyte Li7La3Zr2O12 to form the contamination layers that harm its performance.25 Since no single electrolyte is stable against both a high-voltage cathode and a metal anode, we screen pairs of electrolytes for bilayer separators, such as the stable Li3PO4/Li3InCl6,26 and test how chloride catholytes and thiophosphate anolytes react with each other.27
